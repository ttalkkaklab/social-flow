# Make the next development worth waiting for

Apply this authoring pass to every new or substantially revised synopsis and storyboard.
For a small edit, inspect its affected setup, response and payoff. It is the normal writing
process, not a new schema, numerical gate, automatic reviewer or permission to generate media.
The aim is a reason to continue within this episode. A standalone ending pays its promise;
an explicit serial may then open another question. No compulsory cliffhanger or twist.

## 1. Make the promise concrete

Start with a readable situation and something the viewer wants answered or accomplished.
Name the observable discrepancy, desired outcome or unfinished action: a supposedly unwanted
gift is protected; an attempt to help makes the situation harder. Establish the relationship
and immediate context economically so the viewer can form a guess. A vague secret or the
claim that something surprising will happen gives no basis for anticipation.

Know the actual ending before presenting the synopsis. A title or teaser can withhold it
from an audience; the author-facing synopsis includes the resolution and resulting behavior.

### A provocative opening, then the earlier situation

When the user chooses this format for a series or the channel already specifies it, make
it the series default. An episode-only choice applies to that episode alone. Open on an
actual consequential line or action, show the recipient's immediate reaction, then go
briefly back to the situation that caused it. Otherwise consider it alongside chronological
telling; do not impose it on every channel or factual format. Read channel guidelines before
choosing the opening and distinguish the incident from the explanation of its motive.

- **Open on the event.** Put the surprising invitation, accusation, refusal or discovery
  in the first roughly three seconds when it can be performed naturally; on long-form, inside
  the first seven seconds that confirm the title and thumbnail ([opening by format](opening-by-format.md#6-story-led-and-claim-led-openings)). Let the listener's
  visible shock, hesitation or return question finish the opening before the time jump.
  Do not rush two speakers into three seconds or replace the reaction with an author note.
- **Orient the rewind.** Work out the real chronology first. An “eight hours earlier”
  transition must agree with the time of day, place, props and subsequent return. A time
  label is allowed only when the user/channel authorizes on-screen text; otherwise use a
  legible change of setting, prop state or sound. An authorized time label does not permit
  dialogue subtitles or other overlays. Do not silently choose a different wording or
  a vague recollection when the user requested a specific earlier situation.
- **Dramatize the context.** Show the behavior that makes the opening surprising: a strict
  manager actually corrects this employee's mistake before later offering unexpected care.
  A colleague calling the manager frightening is not a substitute for that requested scene.
  Keep the recipient's response and subsequent effort visible; do not turn strictness into
  arbitrary cruelty merely to make the hook louder.
- **Catch up once.** Show only the causes the viewer needs, return through a recognizable
  action or object state, then continue with a new response or consequence. Do not replay
  the whole invitation and surprise, or spend the rewind on biography and logistics.
- **Pay the same promise.** The opening must really happen with the same participants,
  meaning and stakes. Do not erase it as a dream, bait with an unrelated sexual implication
  or explain it away as a different event. A held romantic reveal may show the invitation
  first while saving why it was offered, the confession and the answer for the climax.
  Channel-specific disclosure rules take precedence over the general known-ending option.

Before writing shots, state both event chronology and viewer presentation order in ordinary
scenario prose. Carry the opening, reaction, rewind cue, enacted context and catch-up into
the performed scene sequence. After catch-up, each meaningful beat should change the guess,
choice or consequence; do not stretch the same uncertainty with repeated blushes or retreats.
When clues make affection likely, let the recipient test it or face a consequential choice
without inventing certainty about the other person's motives. Keep the response and a short
changed-behavior ending. Time the spoken hook and its reaction separately; three seconds is
an opening target, not permission to truncate speech or a claim of measured retention.

If the channel also chooses a provocative question-title style, combine a recognizable
relationship or character trait, the concrete unexpected action and a question. Use the
user's preferred role wording. The title and opening must promise the same actual event;
do not turn an invitation into an order or reveal the romantic answer merely for a stronger
headline. Store the style in channel guidelines and update the episode's visible title,
scenario and review metadata together. A question title is not a substitute for the scene.

## 2. Change the expectation while giving something back

At each meaningful beat, track this in prose or existing planning notes:

| Going in | What actually happens | Going out |
|---|---|---|
| What does the viewer expect, want or fear? | Which clue, attempt, response or consequence changes that expectation? | What specifically do they now want to see, and why does this event lead there? |

Let the story determine the number of beats. A question can persist while progress changes
the likely answer, the cost, the available options or the expected form of its fulfillment.
Repeated hesitation, repeated blushing and successive almost-confessions do not provide
progress on their own. Give a partial answer, earned closeness, a useful clue, a failed
prediction or a consequence before asking for more patience. Small rewards need not settle
the central question.

If the audience already knows the outcome, ask how the characters will reach it and show
what makes that route uncertain or worth seeing. Knowing both people are in love is valid;
watching them repeat the same avoidance without a changing choice is weak. Do not add a
contrived rival, interrupted sentence, arbitrary deadline or ignored obvious solution to
manufacture doubt.

## 3. Make the payoff change the meaning or the situation

Plant a concrete action, phrase or object while it does a job in the present scene.
Later evidence may reframe it: apparent indifference was a deliberate accommodation;
an ordinary repeated act was a private invitation. This is one useful method, not a
requirement that every story conceal a secret. A difficult action finally accomplished
or a known fact applied can pay the promise without any reversal.

When using reinterpretation, the earlier evidence must support the later reading. Do not
invalidate what the audience saw or have a narrator announce an unperformed history.
A witness can disclose something they plausibly know, but the leads still make the choice
and respond. An outsider explaining every secret is not the default ending.

After the discovery, show its recipient accepting, refusing, reciprocating or changing a
specific action. Necessary response is part of the payoff, not disposable epilogue. A
returning gesture can now have a different doer, intention or consequence. Stop when that
change lands; a generic smile or a summary that they lived happily adds little by itself.

## Claim-led episodes: one result promised, proof the viewer can check

Use this when the episode's promise is a claim about the world: an explainer, a tutorial, a
review, an advertisement, a documentary that demonstrates something. "This works", "this is
why", "this is how". Any characters in it still follow the sections above; this one says what
the promise and the payoff are when there is no relationship to resolve. The picture choices
stay with [narration and visual grounding](narration-visual-grounding.md) and
[render routing](render-routing.md).

- **Promise one result, in the viewer's words.** Name what the viewer will be able to do,
  know or decide by the end, as they would say it to a friend: "a whole screenplay from one
  line", not the maker's vocabulary of model names, feature counts and versions. One episode
  carries one promise. A list of eight genres and four features gives the viewer nothing
  particular to wait for; pick the case that shows the claim and follow it through.
- **Say it.** Nothing is drawn over video here, so the first spoken line carries the promise;
  a title card or a caption cannot. When the line carries the promise, short-form starts it
  inside the first second and finishes it by about 3 s; long-form says it inside the 0–7 s
  that confirm the title
  ([opening by format](opening-by-format.md#6-story-led-and-claim-led-openings)). Write that line before the shots and keep silent setup in
  front of it short. Once the shots before it have lengths, every board write prints when the
  first spoken line starts (below).
- **The viewer's trouble can come first.** When the subject removes an annoyance the viewer
  already has, showing that annoyance happen is a readable situation and the promise is its
  removal. This is an option for subjects that have one, not a required problem beat.
- **Pay with proof the viewer can check.** A claim about a result is paid by the result on
  screen, in a form the viewer can compare with the promise themselves:
  - what went in is visible in what came out: the typed word appears in the generated
    picture, the measured part is the part that moves;
  - the picture could only belong to this subject. Apply the grounding test: an image that
    could sit under any episode's opening line is not proof;
  - it stays long enough to read or to watch complete. A document scrolled past is a
    gesture at proof.
  A screen or document that needs something pointed at is an HTML slide; a result that is
  itself a moving picture plays as clean footage.
- **Each spoken claim has its picture or its source.** Go through the lines: a claim the
  board cannot show and research cannot support comes out. Numbers and absolutes ("95%",
  "perfect", "completely free") are factual assertions and need their Verified rows
  ([character-led scenarios](character-scenarios.md#choose-content-and-expression-separately));
  a round figure invented for emphasis is a fabricated fact.
- **Give a piece early and change the question.** Show part of the result near the start
  (`reveal: upfront` and its `setups` row in character-scenarios), then let the middle move
  the viewer from whether it works to how far, where it stops or what it costs. A limit shown
  honestly is progress; a second and third example of the same success is repetition.
- **End on someone using it.** The response that completes a claim is application: a person
  does the thing the opening promised, or decides differently because of it. An ask is
  optional. When there is one, it names one action and the place the thing really is, in
  speech, and the publishing copy says the same place.

On the board, `sequence.question` holds the viewer's doubt ("does one line really give a
usable draft?") and `sequence.payoff` names the scene that shows the proof. Once every shot
before the first line has a length, each write prints `first spoken line: <shot>, about N s
in`. For a line the video model voices it adds the seconds that shot's stored length holds
beyond its performance, which play as silence, mostly before the line; a narrator's line is
placed by its composed TTS and gets no such note. It is a reading of the plan, not a
target: an opening carried by a visible event needs no early line.

## 4. Preserve the progression in every artifact

- **Synopsis:** the opening promise, consequential developments, actual payoff and response
  are events in the prose. A list of mood words or an ending hidden from the author is incomplete.
- **Scenario:** write the clue, plausible interpretation, attempted action and resulting
  response in performance order. Respect each character's information and ability to refuse.
- **Storyboard:** keep those cues and their recipients visible or audible. Do not put a
  later revelation into an earlier source image, crop away the decisive object or cut before
  the answer's response. Preserve disclosure order across speaker changes and camera coverage.

Reuse `beats[].expectation`/`change`, `setups`, `sequence.question`/`payoff` and
`scene.turn`/`out` where they already fit. Do not add mandatory keys or write a field merely
to satisfy a label. The audience sees the performance, not the planning notes.

## 5. Read forward and repair the inert stretch

Read only the ordered actions and words. Before each meaningful join, state the specific
thing wanted next and point to the event that created it. If the answer has not changed,
check whether uncertainty, progress or emotional investment has changed. If neither has,
compress the repetition or change the choice and consequence. Do not solve it by merely
speeding up speech, shortening cuts or decorating the same exchange.

Check that the ending answers the opening promise and includes the consequential response.
For a hook-and-rewind draft, also read the opening through the catch-up without author notes:
can the viewer locate the time jump, see why the earlier behavior makes the opening unusual,
and get a new development after catching up? Identify a missing reaction, a mismatched elapsed
time, a repeated opening or a promise the ending never pays by its actual scene/shot.
A quiet reaction or relief can advance acceptance without opening a new question; not every
shot needs a hook. Factual, tutorial, review and advertising episodes read the same way through
[their own promise and proof](#claim-led-episodes-one-result-promised-proof-the-viewer-can-check):
is the promised result named, shown in a form the viewer can check, and put to use? Give
essential answers when useful; never distort facts or delay safety-critical information for
suspense. Length follows meaningful content
and the user's duration constraint, never silence or repeated material used as padding.

This is an editorial assessment of the draft, not proof of retention or an independent
score. Requested independent reviews use an unprimed audience export and retain their
first-pass observations. Public view counts do not establish why an episode was watched.

## Evidence

The [six-video channel analysis](../../../docs/research/2026-11-16-episode-anticipation/README.md)
records observed scenes, approximate timing and limits. It motivates this practical pass;
it does not establish a universal formula. Borrow causal mechanisms, not a creator's lines,
distinctive incident sequence, cast, visual template or unverified claim to a true story.

|---|---|
| State what the viewer gets in the first seconds, and say it aloud | platform guidance for ads (TikTok help, Google ABCDs); not measured on organic Shorts |
| One focused message | platform guidance for ads |
| A promise in the viewer's words reaches further than the maker's vocabulary | observation on one channel's Shorts; other differences were not controlled |
| Proof the viewer can compare with the promise | editorial inference from the same channel and the platform advice to show the product in use |
| An earlier proof earns more views | not supported: on that channel the earliest proof had the fewest views |
| Text overlays help | platform guidance; not adopted, because nothing is drawn over video here |

Whether an opening held viewers is the channel's own "stayed to watch" figure; public view
counts cannot show it. The first-line reading reports the plan and predicts nothing.
